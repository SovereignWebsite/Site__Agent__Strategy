Strategy / Account B / Use case: backups from a twin

strategy.b, account B, 24 November 2026, version 0.1

Use case: your Calendar and Gmail, backed up in your own browser

From Dinis's strategy memo of 24 Nov, about 00:10 UTC: "I'm trying to find these sort of use cases that we can see that we provide a unique capability, because that's what will drive traffic, that's what will drive activities." (the memo, verbatim). Fact marks what a public page says, read 24 Nov 2026; Proposal marks what I suggest.

The capability Proposal

Open a web page, sign in to Google from that page, and your browser builds a copy of your Calendar (then your Gmail) as a digital twin: a graph of your events and messages, version-controlled so every change is kept, encrypted with your own key, and stored in the browser's own storage, later in your vault. While the tab is open it keeps itself up to date. Nothing passes through our servers: the token and the data stay in your browser.

In Dinis's words: "there is no backup for G Calendar effective, but we can create one ... even without a vault ... into local storage using the local twin"; and the twin is "a version controlled, Git version of email", which "can even be encrypted with a user's key".

So a person can export, but by hand, or at best every two months for a year; and anything deleted is gone after 30 days. There is no continuous, versioned copy that the person holds.

The gap: none of the paid services read shows a plan for a personal Google account, and each holds your data on its own servers. The pain is sharpest for people and small teams on personal or small accounts (our segments A and B on the plan): a deleted calendar, a lost account, a mistake an agent made in the inbox, with no copy they own.

Google's own data protection guide calls protection "a shared responsibility; a collaboration between the customer and the Cloud service provider" and says "The customer should have a policy that addresses the requirements for backup, recovery, and restoration" (Workspace Data Protection Guide, Dec 2020). I found no Google page that says a person must back up their own data; the stronger claims come from backup vendors.

The rule that decides the order Fact

  • Reading Calendar is a sensitive scope: "Examples of sensitive scopes include reading events stored in Google Calendar" (Google for Developers). A published app needs Google's verification.
  • Reading Gmail (gmail.readonly) is a restricted scope (Gmail scopes). An app whose restricted data goes "from or through a third-party server" needs a security assessment under CASA, "at least every 12 months" (restricted-scope verification).

So Calendar first, as Dinis said, is also the cheaper door. Whether a browser-only app, with no server of ours, still needs the yearly assessment is not settled by the pages I read; it needs checking with Google's terms before Gmail is offered to the public. Dinis's earlier route, where each organisation creates its own Google app in its own domain, moves that burden to them.

How we would tell it Proposal

  1. The line: "Your calendar, backed up in your own browser. Every change kept, encrypted with your key, and nothing on our servers."
  2. The proof first: a page where a visitor presses one button, signs in to Google, and watches their own calendar arrive as a twin, with a history they can scroll back through. No sign-up, nothing sent to us.
  3. The honest lines beside it: what Google already gives (export, Takeout every two months, 30-day trash); that it runs while the tab is open; that Gmail comes later, after Google's verification.
  4. Who hears it first: segment A, founders and small teams, as the demo that shows the agentic workflow is real; then segment B, people, as a free tool that leads to "I want one".
  5. The story for traffic: "The backup Google does not give you." A newsroom article (b.newsroom, when Dinis says go) and the hub linking to the demo.
  6. The component story: the same backup as a small open component any sovereign website can add, which is how sites become useful, not just pretty.

Decisions for Dinis

1. Whose Google app? Ours (we carry Google's verification, and for Gmail the yearly assessment), or each owner's own (they carry it, the fork-it-yourself route).

My recommendation: ours for Calendar (sensitive scope, one verification, the public demo needs it); each owner's own for Gmail until we know whether the yearly assessment applies to a browser-only app.

2. Free or paid? The demo drives traffic only if it is free and needs no sign-up.

My recommendation: free, and the vault copy is what leads to the paid offer later. No price is set here.
